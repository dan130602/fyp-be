# Singlish register guide (version v1, provisional: pending native Singaporean review)

This is reference material for authors and verifiers of SINGLISH calls. It is not a checklist, a dictionary or a quota: there are no mandatory expressions, no required particles and no limits on how many may appear. Use judgement about what a real person would say at that moment.

## Who speaks what

- The client speaks colloquial Singapore English: an English-based register with local grammar, discourse particles and a few established local words. They still sound like a private-banking client, not a caricature.
- The employee speaks warm, professional English with no Singlish phrasing: no particles, no local slang.
- No Chinese characters. No deliberate romanised Mandarin, Hokkien, Cantonese or Malay sentences or phrases (for example "mei ban fa", "xie xie", "hao lah", "tak boleh accept"). A local word that has become part of Singaporean English (kiasu, paiseh, jialat, kena, tahan, agak-agak, bo bian, sian) is fine; it does not count as switching language.

## What makes it read as natural

Register comes mostly from grammar and from how a sentence is shaped, then from particles and a few words, in that order.

- Dropped subjects, dropped "is/are": "This one quite risky." "Can proceed already."
- "Can" / "cannot" as a full answer; "got" for having or existing: "Got fee or not?"
- "Already" and "liao" for completion; repetition for emphasis ("wait wait"); topic first: "This bond ah, how long?"
- Tag questions: "okay or not?", "correct hor?"
- Particles carry a job: lah (firmness, reassurance), lor (resignation), leh (soft contrast), meh (doubt), hor (checking agreement), ah (a question or a softener), sia (surprise), one (emphasis). Put one where it does that job, usually at the end of a clause. Several can appear in a stretch of talk, but never bolted on mechanically or repeated to fill a line. Plenty of natural client lines carry none.
- A few interjections, tied to a real feeling: wah, aiya, aiyoh, alamak, walao. Sparingly.
- Within one call, spell each word one way (lah, lor, leh, meh, hor, sia, liao, walao, aiyoh, paiseh, bo bian, jialat).

## Illustrative vocabulary (ideas only)

Particles: lah, lor, leh, meh, hor, ah, sia, liao, one.
Interjections: wah, aiya, aiyoh, alamak, walao.
Words: paiseh, sian, jialat, kiasu, kiasi, blur, cheem, steady, kena, tahan, agak-agak, bo bian, bo chap.
Phrases: see how first, don't anyhow, like that ah, how ah, where got, can or not.

## A call's content comes first

Colour never changes what an event does. Whatever a planned event is for, the wording keeps that act and that meaning, and values stay in their slots.

- Some wording fits only an event whose planned act it is. "Okay lah, steady, let's proceed." can realise a planned final confirmation; it is not a weak acknowledgement and not filler. "I can tahan some losses." can be planned evidence of accepting loss. "I bo chap the short-term swings." shows indifference and does not, by itself, accept losses. "I thought the returns guaranteed meh?" and "Where got guaranteed returns?" are a client's question or doubt, not an employee's guarantee. "Paiseh, can explain the charges again?" is a client's request; it is not an employee stating a fee. A question is not an instruction, and "can buy another ... anot?" must match the event's real order, with every value in its slot.
- Supporting speech that is not a planned event stays neutral. It adds no confirmation, risk acceptance, guarantee, fee statement, advice or value, and no comment on markets, products or performance. Fitting examples: "Paiseh, line a bit bad just now. Can say again?", "Last time the app very cheem leh, now better already.", "Wah, okay.", "Aiyoh, wait ah."
- A planned weak acknowledgement stays genuinely weak: hedged or non-committal ("Hmm, let me think a bit first ah."). A bare "Can lah." would read as agreement.

## Employee

"You understand the charges hor?" would be wrong for the employee: it is Singlish, and understanding charges is not the understanding of disclosed risks. The employee asks and explains in plain professional English.

## Judging output

A native reader checks authenticity, caricature, whether each event still does its planned job, how smoothly speech joins the planned wording, and whether the employee stayed professional. Counting particles or matching words proves nothing about naturalness.
